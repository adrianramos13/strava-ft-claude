# Age as a modifier of training plans, and HRmax estimation

Scope: exercise only. Training history sets the level; age changes recovery spacing, progression rate and extra components (strength, balance). Evidence-strength tags: [STRONG] = position stand, guideline or meta-analysis; [MODERATE] = consistent small studies; [WEAK] = single small study, expert opinion, or not verified in the primary text.

Verification note: PubMed abstract pages blocked automated fetching (cookie wall). Items marked "(abstract/secondary)" were checked through abstracts, repository listings or secondary summaries, not the full text. Items marked "(not verified)" come from background knowledge and need checking against the primary source before they are used as hard rules.

## 1. Recovery in masters athletes (hours between hard sessions, deload 2:1 vs 3:1)

### Takeaway
In trained masters athletes, measured performance usually recovers at a similar rate, or slightly more slowly, than in young athletes. Perceived recovery (fatigue, soreness, motivation) and some markers (lactate clearance, muscle protein synthesis, power after high-intensity intervals in men about 62) are clearly slower. A sensible default is to add roughly 24 h between hard sessions for 50+ (48 h becomes about 72 h) and to adjust from perceived recovery. No study tests 2:1 against 3:1 deload cycles in masters endurance athletes.

### Cited Findings
- Fell & Williams 2008 (review, J Aging Phys Act 16(1):97-115) argue that older muscle may be more easily damaged and slower to repair. They also say most studies confound aging with lower activity and use protocols that do not look like real training, so the evidence is not conclusive. [WEAK-MODERATE] — [PubMed 18268815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18268815/) (abstract/secondary)
- Borges, Reaburn, Driller & Argus 2016 (narrative review, J Aging Phys Act) conclude that the age effect on recovery may be smaller than once thought, that inactivity may matter more than age, and that research on recovery kinetics is limited. [MODERATE] — [Waikato Research Commons](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/43c52ed0-3e7e-4f32-98fa-ccd16580054b) (abstract/secondary)
- Borges et al. 2018 (Exp Aging Res) compared masters and young cyclists after a high-intensity interval session. Both groups recovered 10-s sprint peak power at similar rates over 48 h, but masters reported more fatigue and soreness and lower motivation at 48 h. The authors warn this may hurt long-term adherence. About 8-10 per group. [MODERATE] — [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/7017a390-b35b-4512-8435-8543773a0af0); [Waikato](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/a48aa9d1-03da-433f-94d9-94295a908a75) (abstract/secondary)
- Borges et al. 2018 (blood lactate): masters cyclists cleared lactate more slowly after both a continuous session and high-intensity intervals (6 x 30 s at 175% of peak power output). The authors suggest more recovery time or active recovery. [MODERATE] — [CQUniversity](https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Blood_lactate_accumulation_and_clearance_during_typical_training_sessions_in_well-trained_masters_and_young_cyclists/24515086)
- Doering et al. 2016 (Med Sci Sports Exerc 48): after a 30-min downhill run, masters triathletes (about 53 y, n=5) had lower myofibrillar protein synthesis than young triathletes (n=6). Their 20-km cycling time trials were done 10, 24 and 48 h after the run, and the masters tended to be slower at 10 h (a trend only). [WEAK, very small n] — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/lower-integrated-muscle-protein-synthesis-in-masters-compared-wit/); [McMaster](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/display/publication1119339)
- Easthope et al. 2010 (Eur J Appl Physiol), 55-km trail race: masters and young runners had similar fatigue and muscle damage, but masters recovered more slowly. [MODERATE] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-011-2115-9) (secondary summary; the DOI listed suggests 2011 online publication)
- Herbert et al. 2015: after one high-intensity session (6 x 30 s sprints), men about 62 needed about 5 days to recover peak power output, while men about 22 needed about 3 days. [MODERATE, small n] — [PMC4359174](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4359174); [UWS research portal](https://research-portal.uws.ac.uk/en/publications/prolonged-recovery-to-a-single-bout-of-high-intensity-interval-tr/)
- The same group used low-frequency high-intensity training in masters athletes of about 60: 9 sessions over 6 weeks (about one every 5 days), which raised peak power output. This shows a low-frequency format works for this age group. [MODERATE] — [Hayes et al., Cumbria repository](https://insight.cumbria.ac.uk/3181/3/Hayes_HIITProducesIncreases.pdf); [Knowles et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4359174)
- After an Olympic-distance triathlon, fatigue at 24 h appeared to be independent of age in well-trained athletes. [WEAK, secondary] — [Springer s40279-013-0067-4](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-013-0067-4)
- Resistance training: ACSM (Garber 2011) recommends at least 48 h between sessions for the same muscle group, at all ages. [STRONG] — [Kravitz summary of Garber et al. 2011](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)

### Inferences
- Default hard-to-hard spacing for endurance training (intervals, threshold, long runs or rides):
  - under 35: 48 h
  - 35-49: 48 h, or 72 h if perceived recovery is poor
  - 50-64: 72 h
  - 65+: 72-96 h
- For all-out sprint-type high-intensity work at 60+, consider one session every 5 days, based on Herbert 2015.
- These are starting defaults, based on small studies. Use the athlete's own readiness (RPE, soreness, resting HR or HRV if available) to shorten or lengthen them.
- Deload: there is no direct trial of 2:1 vs 3:1. A defensible app rule is to keep 3:1 by default and offer 2:1 for 50+ when (a) the plan includes 2 or more hard sessions per week, (b) the athlete's history is short, or (c) the athlete reports poor recovery. This is expert-opinion level.
- Because masters show slower perceived recovery while performance recovery is similar, the app should not cut workload in advance just because of age. It should space hard days and let feedback drive further changes.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares 2:1 and 3:1 mesocycles in masters endurance athletes.
- All direct recovery studies have about 5-15 participants per group and are mostly male. Data for women are sparse.
- The Fell & Williams and Easthope full texts were not retrieved.

## 2. Should weekly volume progression be slower for older or untrained older adults?

### Takeaway
Guidelines give qualitative rules ("gradual", "low at the outset", "conservative for the deconditioned") but no age-specific percentage per week. Concrete numbers exist only for strength starting loads and step counts. Any weekly percentage cap for older adults is expert opinion.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AHA, as summarized in Chodzko-Zajko 2009: for highly deconditioned, functionally limited or chronically ill older adults, intensity and duration should be low at first, progression should be individual and tailored to tolerance and preference, and a conservative approach may be needed for the most deconditioned. [STRONG for the principle; no numbers] — [Chodzko-Zajko et al. 2009, MSSE 41(7):1510-30 (PDF)](https://www.nescon.medicina.ufmg.br/biblioteca/imagem/4467.pdf)
- Garber 2011: "Progress intensity, duration and frequency gradually until desired goal is attained". It also says that gradual progression of intensity and volume may reduce the risks of exercise. [STRONG, qualitative] — [Kravitz summary](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf); [PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- Garber 2011: when using a pedometer, raise daily steps by about 2,000 at a time, toward at least 7,000 steps/day. [STRONG-MODERATE] — [Kravitz summary](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)
- Garber 2011, starting strength load: 40-50% 1RM ("very light to light") for beginning older or sedentary people, 10-15 reps, single set. [STRONG] — [Kravitz summary](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)
- ACSM 2015 screening: inactive people without disease or symptoms may start light-to-moderate exercise and "gradually progress per ACSM guidelines". [STRONG] — [Whitfield et al. 2017, PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- The commonly quoted ACSM increment "+5-10 min per session every 1-2 weeks during the first 4-6 weeks" could not be confirmed in the 2011 position stand text that was retrieved. It probably comes from ACSM's Guidelines for Exercise Testing and Prescription. (not verified)

### Inferences
- Suggested app rule (expert opinion, not age-tested):
  - Trained athletes: use the same progression cap at all ages (for example, about 10% per week or less). Age only adds spacing between hard sessions and more frequent deloads.
  - Untrained or returning people aged 50+: build session duration first (about +5 min per session per week), then frequency, and only then intensity. Keep intensity moderate (RPE 12-13 on the 6-20 scale) for the first 4-8 weeks.
  - Untrained 65+: start at light-to-moderate intensity (RPE 5-6 on a 0-10 scale) and progress more conservatively, for example about 5% per week.
- Order of progression for sedentary older adults: duration, then frequency, then intensity. This matches the ACSM wording "intensity and duration ... low at the outset".

### Gaps
- No trial-derived weekly percentage cap specific to older adults was found. The 10% rule itself has weak evidence (no source retrieved in this research).

## 3. ACSM and WHO guidelines for older adults: strength, balance, aerobic minutes

### Takeaway
For everyone 65+, use aerobic activity of 150-300 min/week moderate or 75-150 min/week vigorous, muscle strengthening on 2 or more days/week, and (WHO 2020) multicomponent balance plus strength work on 3 or more days/week. ACSM sets strength parameters at 8-10 exercises x 8-12 reps, starting at 40-50% 1RM for beginners. Exercise programmes centred on balance and function reduce falls by about 23%.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020, adults and older adults: 150-300 min/week moderate aerobic activity, or 75-150 min/week vigorous, or an equivalent mix. Muscle-strengthening at moderate or greater intensity for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days/week. [STRONG] — [Bull et al. 2020 summary, PMC9219310](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9219310); [Univ. Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2020/11/26/new-global-guidelines-on-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavio.html)
- WHO 2020, 65+ only: on 3 or more days/week, varied multicomponent activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity. This was new in 2020 and applies to all older adults, not only those with poor mobility as in 2010. [STRONG] — [Univ. Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2020/11/26/new-global-guidelines-on-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavio.html); [Croakey](https://www.croakey.org/who-2020-guidelines-on-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behaviour-what-is-new-and-why-it-matters/)
- WHO 2020: if chronic conditions prevent reaching 150 min, older adults should be as active as their abilities allow. [STRONG] — [Univ. Sydney](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2020/11/26/new-global-guidelines-on-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavio.html)
- ACSM/AHA older adults (Chodzko-Zajko 2009, Table 1):
  - Aerobic: moderate effort is 5-6 and vigorous is 7-8 on a 0-10 scale. At least 30 min/day of moderate activity in bouts of 10 min or more, or at least 20 min/day of continuous vigorous activity.
  - Modality: avoid excessive orthopedic stress. Walking is typical; aquatic or stationary cycling suits people with limited weight-bearing tolerance.
  - Resistance: at least 2 days/week, intensity 5-6 to 7-8 out of 10, 8-10 exercises for major muscle groups, 8-12 reps each.
  - Flexibility: at least 2 days/week at intensity 5-6.
  - Balance: for frequent fallers or people with mobility problems. Use progressively harder postures (two-legged, then semi-tandem, tandem, one-legged), dynamic moves (tandem walk, circle turns), heel and toe stands, and eyes closed. No specific dose was given because of limited evidence.
  - [STRONG] — [Chodzko-Zajko 2009 PDF](https://www.nescon.medicina.ufmg.br/biblioteca/imagem/4467.pdf)
- ACSM Garber 2011, resistance:
  - Each muscle group 2-3 days/week, with 48 h between sessions for the same group.
  - Load: 40-50% 1RM for beginning older or sedentary people; 60-70% for novice to intermediate; 80% or more for experienced lifters. Power work for older adults at 20-50% 1RM.
  - Reps: 10-15 for beginning, middle-aged and older people.
  - Sets: a single set for novices and older adults; 2-4 sets for most adults.
  - [STRONG] — [Kravitz summary of Garber 2011](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)
- ACSM Garber 2011, neuromotor (balance, agility, gait): 2-3 days/week, 20-30 min/day, recommended especially for older adults. Intensity not determined. [MODERATE] — [Kravitz summary](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)
- Garber 2011, aerobic for all adults: 30 min or more on 5 or more days/week moderate (150 min/week or more), or 20 min or more on 3 or more days/week vigorous. Moderate is RPE 12-13 and vigorous is RPE 14-17 on the 6-20 scale. [STRONG] — [PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/); [Kravitz](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/ACSMGuidelinesUNM.pdf)
- Sherrington 2019 Cochrane review (age 60+, community-dwelling, 59 studies, n=12,981): exercise reduced the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.71-0.83, high certainty) and the number of people who fell by 15%. Programmes that mainly use balance and functional exercise had high-certainty benefit. [STRONG] — [Cochrane news](https://www.cochrane.org/zh-hant/about-us/news/new-cochrane-review-assesses-benefits-and-harms-exercise-preventing-falls-older-people-living); [world.physio WHO review](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/evidence-physical-activity-and-falls-prevention-people-aged-65-years-systematic)
- The NSCA position statement on resistance training for older adults (Fragala et al. 2019, J Strength Cond Res 33(8):2019-2052) covers program design, frailty and sarcopenia. Its numbers could not be retrieved; it is commonly cited as 2-3 days/week, 1-3 sets, 70-85% 1RM, plus power work at lighter loads (not verified). [STRONG source, numbers not verified] — [PubMed 31343601](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/)
- In 2026 ACSM published a new resistance-training position stand, the first update since 2009. It may supersede the 2009 progression models. Not reviewed here. — [Club Solutions 2026](https://clubsolutionsmagazine.com/2026/04/acsm-has-a-new-position-stand-for-resistance-training-heres-what-you-need-to-know/)

### Inferences
- App rules by age group:
  - 65+: 2 strength sessions/week (8-10 exercises, 1-2 sets of 10-15 reps, building from about 40-50% 1RM or RPE 5-6 toward RPE 7-8). Ensure 3 or more days/week include balance or multicomponent work; 10-15 min of balance drills inside sessions can count.
  - 50-64: 2 strength sessions/week. Offer optional balance or neuromotor work 2-3 days/week (ACSM Garber).
  - Under 50: 2 strength sessions/week as WHO's general adult recommendation. Optional, not mandatory, for performance plans.
- Endurance sessions count toward the aerobic minutes. A trained 65+ endurance athlete will already exceed 150-300 min, so the age-specific additions are strength and balance, not more aerobic work.

### Gaps
- Fragala 2019 and the 2026 ACSM stand were not read in full.
- No dose-response for balance minutes/week was confirmed. The "3 h/week" figure from the earlier Sherrington 2017 analysis was not verified.

## 4. Pre-participation screening (ACSM 2015): what a non-medical app should say

### Takeaway
ACSM 2015 (Riebe et al., MSSE 47(11):2473-79) screens on three things: current exercise habit, known cardiovascular, metabolic or renal disease, and symptoms. Age alone is not a trigger. An app can apply it as a self-report questionnaire and route people to "see a doctor before starting" or "start light to moderate and progress gradually".

### Cited Findings
- "Currently exercising" means planned, structured exercise of at least moderate intensity, 30 min or more on 3 or more days/week, for at least the last 3 months. [STRONG] — [Whitfield et al. 2017, PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- Disease categories: cardiac, peripheral vascular or cerebrovascular disease; type 1 or type 2 diabetes; renal disease. [STRONG] — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- Symptoms to check: chest, neck, jaw or arm pain or discomfort; shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion; dizziness or fainting; shortness of breath when lying flat or at night; ankle swelling; palpitations or a racing heart; calf pain when walking (claudication); known heart murmur; unusual fatigue or breathlessness with usual activities. [STRONG] — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- Decision matrix [STRONG] — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860):
  - Not exercising, no disease, no symptoms: start light-to-moderate and progress gradually.
  - Not exercising, with disease or symptoms: get medical clearance before any exercise.
  - Exercising, no disease, no symptoms: continue at moderate or vigorous intensity and progress gradually.
  - Exercising, with disease but no symptoms: continue light-to-moderate; get clearance before vigorous exercise (clearance within the last 12 months is acceptable if nothing has changed).
  - Any symptoms: stop and get medical clearance.
- Intensity bands:
  - Light: 30 to under 40% HRR, 2 to under 3 METs, RPE 9-11.
  - Moderate: 40 to under 60% HRR, 3 to under 6 METs, RPE 12-13.
  - Vigorous: 60% HRR or more, 6 METs or more, RPE 14 or higher.
  - [STRONG] — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- The 2015 update removed age and risk-factor counts from screening. In one analysis, physician referrals fell by about 41%; whether the algorithm correctly identifies people at risk still needs validation. [MODERATE] — [ACE summary](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/pros/expert-articles/6921/new-preparticipation-screening-guidelines-what-health-and-fitness-pros-need-to-know/); [NFPT](https://nfpt.com/assessing-cardiovascular-risk-with-fitness-clients/)

### Inferences
- App text: ask the 3 questions (exercise habit, diagnosed heart, vascular, diabetes or kidney disease, and the symptom list).
  - If any symptom is present: "Stop and consult a doctor before training."
  - If disease is present and the plan includes vigorous sessions (60% HRR or more, or RPE 14 or higher): "Get medical clearance before high-intensity sessions. Until then, the plan is limited to light-to-moderate intensity."
  - Always add a disclaimer that the app does not give medical advice, and a rule to stop exercising if chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness occurs.
- Do not gate users on age alone; ACSM 2015 removed that.

### Gaps
- The original Riebe 2015 paper was not fetched; the details above come from Whitfield 2017 (same author group) and secondary sources.

## 5. HRmax estimation from age

### Takeaway
Use Tanaka (208 − 0.7 × age) or Nes (211 − 0.64 × age) instead of 220 − age. All age formulas have an error (SEE or SD) of about 10-12 bpm. About 95% of people fall within ±20 bpm of the estimate, so a measured value (from a test or the highest reliable HR seen in hard races or sessions) should always override the estimate.

### Cited Findings
- Tanaka, Monahan & Seals 2001 (JACC 37:153-6) meta-analysis: HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age. Reported as independent of sex and activity level. [STRONG] — [PubMed 11153730](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153730/). Details not verified here: 351 studies and 18,712 subjects, a lab validation in 514 people, and 220 − age overestimating HRmax in young adults and underestimating it in older adults (the gap grows after about 40).
- Gellish et al. 2007 (MSSE, longitudinal, 908 tests in 132 people, mixed models): HRmax = 207 − 0.7 × age. Its introduction cites an SD of about 10-12 bpm for age-based estimates. Results are almost identical to Tanaka. [STRONG-MODERATE] — [Scribd copy of Gellish 2007](https://www.scribd.com/document/606670952/gellish2007); [PMC7523886 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7523886/table/t2-ijes-13-7-1242)
- Nes et al. 2013 (HUNT Fitness Study, Scand J Med Sci Sports 23(6):697-704; n=3,320 healthy adults aged 19-89, maximal effort verified): HRmax = 211 − 0.64 × age, SEE 10.8 bpm. No interaction with sex, physical activity, VO2max or BMI. [STRONG] — [PubMed 22376273](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22376273/)
- An independent validation (Int J Exerc Sci 13(7), PMC7523886) found significant differences between measured and predicted HRmax for several equations, including Nes. [MODERATE] — [PMC7523886](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7523886)
- Worked values (computed from the formulas):

  | Age | 220 − age | Tanaka | Gellish | Nes |
  |-----|-----------|--------|---------|-----|
  | 30 | 190 | 187 | 186 | 192 |
  | 50 | 170 | 173 | 172 | 179 |
  | 70 | 150 | 159 | 158 | 166 |

  After about 40, 220 − age underestimates HRmax, which would make every zone too low for older athletes.

### Inferences
- Default to Nes (larger sample, healthy, mostly European population, verified maximal effort) or Tanaka. They differ by about 5-7 bpm across adult ages, which is well within the error band.
- Show the estimate with a ±10-11 bpm SEE and label it "estimated".
- Prefer HR-reserve (Karvonen) zones using measured resting HR, or threshold-based zones (LTHR or field tests), over %HRmax from a formula.
- Override the estimate when the athlete's data show a higher HR during clearly maximal efforts, for example in Strava streams: take the 99th-percentile peak from hard races, after filtering out artefacts.
- Beta-blockers and some other medications blunt heart rate, so formula-based HR zones are invalid for those users. Use RPE instead (based on ACSM's general cautions; not sourced here).
- HRmax falls by about 0.64-0.7 bpm per year of age (the slopes above). Training does not change it much (Nes found no interaction with activity level). Re-estimate each year from age, or keep the measured value with a decay of about 0.7 bpm per year when no new maximal data exist.

### Gaps
- Tanaka's study and subject counts, and the 220 − age bias by age, were not verified from the abstract because PubMed was blocked.
- No SD specific to the Gellish equation was found. Its "±7 bpm" figure conflicts with the common 10-12 bpm range.

## 6. Should age itself reduce intensity or volume in trained people?

### Takeaway
No guideline says to lower intensity or volume because of age alone in healthy trained people. The evidence says keeping training volume and intensity up is what limits VO2max decline. Age should change recovery spacing, deload frequency, and the addition of strength and balance work, not cap intensity or volume. Health status (screening) and training history should drive any reductions.

### Cited Findings
- Masters athletes who kept their training lost about 5.5% of VO2max per decade, compared with about 12% per decade in sedentary men (Rogers 1990; small n of 15 and 14). [MODERATE] — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/decline-in-vo2-max-with-aging-in-master-athletes-and-sedentary-me/)
- A 2022 review (IJERPH 19:11050): masters who maintained training volume (no more than a 10% reduction) lost about 5-6.5% VO2max per decade. Those who cut to moderate training lost up to about 26%, and those who became sedentary up to about 46%. Sedentary adults lose about 10% per decade after 25-30. [MODERATE] — [doi 10.3390/ijerph191711050](https://doi.org/10.3390%2Fijerph191711050); [PMC9517884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9517884)
- Pimentel 2003 found a faster absolute decline in endurance-trained than in sedentary men, partly because trained men start higher and many reduce training. [MODERATE, conflicting] — [PMC9517884](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9517884)
- Borges 2016: inactivity may matter more than age for recovery; systematic training preserves performance capacity. [MODERATE] — [Waikato](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/43c52ed0-3e7e-4f32-98fa-ccd16580054b)
- Six weeks of high-intensity intervals (9 sessions) raised peak power output and free testosterone in masters men aged about 60. High intensity is well tolerated and effective when spaced. [MODERATE] — [Hayes et al.](https://insight.cumbria.ac.uk/3181/3/Hayes_HIITProducesIncreases.pdf)
- ACSM 2015 screening lets asymptomatic, disease-free exercisers continue vigorous exercise with no age limit. [STRONG] — [PMC7059860](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860)
- ACSM/AHA 2009: lower intensity at the outset applies to highly deconditioned, functionally limited or chronically ill older adults, not to all older adults. [STRONG] — [Chodzko-Zajko 2009](https://www.nescon.medicina.ufmg.br/biblioteca/imagem/4467.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rule set for the generator (history sets level and volume; age only modulates):

  | Age | Hard-session spacing | Deload | Max hard sessions/week | Added components |
  |-----|---------------------|--------|------------------------|------------------|
  | Under 35 | 48 h | 3:1 | 3 | Strength optional (2/wk recommended) |
  | 35-49 | 48 h (72 h if poorly recovered) | 3:1 | 2-3 | Strength 2/wk |
  | 50-64 | about 72 h | 3:1, or 2:1 if recovery is poor or history is short | 2 | Strength 2/wk; balance optional |
  | 65+ | 72-96 h | 2:1 option | 2 (sprint-type high-intensity work at most 1 per 5 days) | Strength 2/wk + multicomponent balance 3 or more days/wk (WHO) |

  All of these are expert-opinion syntheses of small studies, not trial-validated thresholds.
- Untrained people of any age: progression follows history. Untrained 50+ and 65+ start at light-moderate intensity (ACSM screening plus Chodzko-Zajko) with slower increases.
- Avoid ageist over-restriction: do not lower target zones, cap long-session duration, or remove intensity for trained 50+ or 65+ people on age alone. Only spacing changes.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests age-based intensity caps in trained masters athletes. Women masters athletes are under-represented in all of the cited evidence.
- Evidence on masters injury risk and maximum tolerable weekly increase was not found in this research.
